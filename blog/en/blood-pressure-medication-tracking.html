Blood Pressure Medication: A Practical Guide to Taking It Regularly

Taking a medicine every day for a condition you can't feel: a fixed time, a record, and a refill before the box runs out.

← All Articles

Bardino · 15 October 2026

There is a practical formula for taking blood pressure medication regularly: tie it to the same moment every day, mark it off when you take it, and get the next box before the current one runs out. Which medicine you take, at what dose and at what time is your doctor's decision. This article is about carrying out that decision in everyday life without gaps.

Why "I feel fine" isn't a measure

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 1.4 billion adults aged 30-79 had hypertension in 2024. Of these, 44% were unaware of it, and 23% had it under control.[1] WHO also says that most people with hypertension don't feel any symptoms.[1] Turkey's Ministry of Health, through its General Directorate of Public Health, calls high blood pressure a "silent enemy" because it can damage the kidneys, brain, heart and blood vessels for a long time without causing symptoms.[2]

This has a direct consequence for taking your medicine: most of the time you won't feel any difference between a day you took it and a day you didn't. The NHS page on one blood pressure medicine, ramipril, says so plainly: if you take it for high blood pressure you may not feel any different, but this does not mean the medicine is not working, and it's important to keep taking it. The same page says that even if ramipril has lowered your blood pressure, it's best to carry on taking it, because if you stop, your blood pressure could rise again.[3]

What applies to your own medicine is its leaflet and what your doctor tells you. The general advice points the same way: WHO advises taking medicines as prescribed by your health care professional and not missing doses,[1] and Turkey's Ministry of Health asks that medicines be taken at the recommended dose and for the recommended length of time, without gaps.[2]

Morning or evening?

This is one of the most common questions. In the TIME study, carried out in the UK and published in The Lancet in 2022, 21,104 adults taking blood pressure medication were randomly assigned to take all their usual blood pressure medicines either in the morning or in the evening. People who took their blood pressure medicines at more than one time of day were not included. Over a median follow-up of 5.2 years, the main outcome (death from vascular causes, or a hospital admission for a non-fatal heart attack or stroke) occurred in 3.4% of the evening group and 3.7% of the morning group; the difference was not statistically significant.[4] The authors conclude that patients can be advised to take their regular blood pressure medicines at a convenient time that keeps any unwanted effects to a minimum.[4]

That doesn't mean timing never matters. For some medicines the time is set for a practical reason. For example, the NHS describes bendroflumethiazide as a diuretic used to treat high blood pressure, and says it's usually taken once a day in the morning, because if you take it later in the day you may have to wake up in the night to go to the toilet.[5][6] Your own medicine's leaflet may carry a note like this.

In practice

If your leaflet and your doctor haven't set a particular time, choose one that suits you and that you can keep every day, then stick to it. If you want to change the time, talk to your doctor about that too.

The same routine every day: four steps

1. Tie the medicine to a fixed event

According to the NHS, most blood pressure medicines are taken as tablets once a day, and you may need more than one medicine to keep your blood pressure under control.[7] Tying a once-a-day medicine to something you do every day without fail makes it easier to keep track: brushing your teeth, sitting down to breakfast, the evening news. We explain the technique in our article on building medication into your morning routine. If the medicine has to be taken with food or on an empty stomach, choose your anchor with that in mind; your leaflet will tell you.

2. Mark it when you take it

Don't leave the answer to "Did I take it this morning?" to memory. A paper chart, a weekly pill organiser or an app can do the job. If you're thinking of a weekly pill organiser, ask your pharmacist first; the NHS notes that these boxes are not suitable for every type of medicine.[8]

3. Get the next box before this one runs out

The day the medicine runs out is the day the routine breaks. Our stock tracking article covers ways to keep count of the tablets you have left and renew your prescription in time. If you take more than one blood pressure medicine, or other medicines as well, our guide to keeping track of multiple medications helps you organise the list.

4. Plan for irregular days

Travel, night shifts, staying with friends: days when the clock doesn't work make it harder to keep track. On those days a place can be a better trigger than a time; a location-based reminder (in Dozi, part of Dozi Extra) reminds you when you get home. If you do miss a dose, what to do depends on your medicine: our article "I missed a dose" explains where to look.

Stopping, cutting down, switching: who decides?

When you notice a side effect, or your readings improve, it can be tempting to stop the medicine or lower the dose. Make that decision with your doctor. In our missed dose article we show where the section on stopping treatment sits in the leaflet. Ways to note down side effects so you can take them to your doctor are in our side effect tracking article.

Can diet and exercise replace the medicine?

WHO says lifestyle changes can help lower high blood pressure and can help anyone with hypertension; among them are a healthy, low-salt diet, losing weight, being physically active and quitting tobacco. The same text adds that many people who make these changes will still need to take medicine.[1] If you've changed your lifestyle, treat it as progress to discuss with your doctor, not as a reason to stop the medicine. Lowering the dose or stopping is a decision your doctor makes by looking at your readings.

Home readings: alongside the medicine, not instead of it

If your doctor has asked you to check your blood pressure at home, the method the NHS describes is simple: take your blood pressure, note down the numbers, wait 1 minute and take it again. You usually do this twice a day, once in the morning and once in the evening, for as many days as your doctor or nurse tells you. Machines can differ slightly, so follow the instructions for your own monitor.[9]

If you also note in your readings log when you took your medicine, your doctor sees both at your next check-up. Don't change your dose yourself based on a reading; readings are information collected so that your doctor can decide.
