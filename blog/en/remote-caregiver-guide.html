A Guide for Remote Caregivers: How Do I Keep Track of My Mother's Medicines?

Talk first, then the list, then the tools. Setting up medicine tracking for a relative in another city without wearing them out.

← All Articles

Bardino · 15 October 2026

To keep track of your mother's medicines when she lives in another city, you need four things: her agreement, an up-to-date medicines list, a routine in which she can take her medicines herself, and a system that tells you when something slips. The order matters: first the conversation, then the list, and only then the tools.

We say "mother" in this article, but the same steps apply to a father, a grandparent or any relative who lives alone.

Talk first: support, not supervision

When you raise the subject, the aim is to make things easier for your mother, not to take the job away from her. NICE, the body that issues health guidance in England, writes in its guideline on managing medicines for adults who receive social care in the community that many people want to take an active part in their own care, and that enabling and supporting them to manage their medicines is an essential part of this. According to the guideline, an assessment should focus on how the person can be supported to manage their own medicines.[1]

Privacy is part of the conversation too. The NHS reminds carers that the person they care for has a right to confidentiality, and that it's up to them to decide how much of their health and medicines information their carer can see. The same page says never to give medicine to someone without their consent or try to force them to take it; people have the right to refuse, and if they do, you should talk to their GP or pharmacist.[2]

Concrete questions make the conversation easier:

  • Which medicine do you struggle with: remembering it, opening the box, reading the label? (NICE also says an assessment should look at what the person is able to do, such as reading medicine labels, and what support they need.[1])
  • Would you like me to remind you every day, or should I only hear about it when a dose is missed?
  • Which of my brothers and sisters should be involved?
  • Would you like me to be there when you talk to the doctor or the pharmacist?

Make the medicines list together

On your next visit, put all the boxes on the table. Write down each medicine's name, dose, timing, what it's for and who prescribed it, and add vitamins, supplements and over-the-counter medicines. We explain how to keep the list, and why it matters, in our guide to keeping track of multiple medications.

Going through the list with a pharmacist or doctor may be one of the best uses of your time as a remote carer. The NHS advises carers to try to attend the medicines review with the person they look after.[2] In Turkey, the Good Pharmacy Practice Guidelines of TİTCK, the country's medicines agency, expect the pharmacist to inform not only the patient but also "those who give the patient their medicines" about the dose, dosing times, and the foods and other medicines to avoid around a dose, and to ask which medicines the patient is taking.[3] If you can't be there in person, you could try joining the conversation by phone, with your mother's agreement.

Things to ask: the timing of each medicine, which ones are taken on an empty stomach or with food, what to do if a dose is missed, and which symptoms should be reported to the doctor. We cover the general picture on missed doses in a separate article.

Ways to remind someone from a distance

For someone who often forgets their medicines, the NHS suggests phoning them when it's time to take a medicine, arranging for care workers to visit at medicine times, and getting an automatic pill dispenser that beeps when a dose is due. Elsewhere on the same page it notes that other medicine reminders, such as automated dispensers, specially labelled packets and smartphone apps, are available, and suggests asking a pharmacist about them.[2] Weekly pill organisers are common too, but the NHS says they're not suitable for every type of medicine,[2] and NICE recommends considering such monitored dosage systems only after an assessment by a health professional, for example a pharmacist.[1]

Two rules to share with your mother

  • Making up for a missed dose by taking a double dose later can be dangerous.[2]
  • Over-the-counter medicines shouldn't be taken with prescribed medicines unless a doctor or pharmacist has confirmed it's safe.[2]

Setting it up with Dozi and Badi

The details in this section come from the site's own pages (the FAQ and the home page). We don't add anything that isn't written there.

  1. Phone. Dozi is currently available on Android and is downloaded from Google Play; the site says work on an iOS version is under way. The person following as a Badi uses the same Dozi app, and since the app is Android-only for now, that applies to your phone too.
  2. Medicines. You add the medicines on your mother's phone, with a separate reminder time and dose information for each. Stock tracking, and adding a medicine by showing its box to the camera, are part of Dozi Ekstra. Check what you've added against the prescription.
  3. Badi connection. The connection is made with an invitation code generated in the app.
  4. What will you see? Badis see the names, reminder times and taken status of the medicines they follow. If a medicine isn't taken within the set time, Badis are notified automatically.
  5. Brothers and sisters. More than one person can be a Badi, and everyone sees the current status at the same time.
  6. Internet. Reminders work offline, but Badi synchronisation needs an internet connection, so your mother's phone needs mobile data or Wi-Fi switched on.
  7. Stock (Dozi Ekstra). If the number of tablets in the box is entered when the medicine is added, Dozi counts down with each dose taken and warns when stock runs low.

When a notification arrives

If you agree in advance what to do when a "dose not taken" notification arrives, you won't have to decide on the spot. Settle these questions first:

  • Who calls when a notification comes in? If there's more than one Badi, divide the work.
  • What to do about a missed dose is set out in the leaflet for your mother's medicine and by her pharmacist. You can check it together on the phone.
  • If you can't reach your mother, who will you contact: a neighbour, a relative who lives nearby?
  • If you're worried about her condition, don't hesitate to call the local emergency number.

Dozi shows whether your mother has marked a dose as taken. It doesn't show whether she took the medicine correctly or how she's feeling. It doesn't replace a phone call; it helps you know what to ask when you call.

Count your own load too

Caring from a distance is something you fit into the same days as your work, your children and your own health. Sharing the tracking with your siblings, taking turns to respond to notifications, and trusting the routine you've set up instead of asking the same question every day all make it easier to keep going.
