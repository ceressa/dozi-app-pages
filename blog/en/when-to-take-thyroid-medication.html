When Should You Take Thyroid Medication? Making It Part of Your Morning Routine

The rule official sources share for levothyroxine, and how to follow it every morning.

← All Articles

Bardino · 15 October 2026

For levothyroxine, the medicine used for an underactive thyroid, the general rule shared by official sources is: once a day, on an empty stomach, before the first meal of the day (usually breakfast). How long before breakfast varies between sources, from half an hour to an hour. What applies to you is what your own medicine's leaflet says and what your doctor tells you. This article is about following that rule every morning without forgetting; it doesn't set your time or your dose.

By "thyroid medication" we mean levothyroxine here. If your box names a different active ingredient, the rules below may not apply to your medicine; check its leaflet.

What do the sources say?

  • NHS: take it on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before your first meal of the day, before any drink with caffeine such as tea or coffee, and before any other medicine; try to take it at the same time each day, and swallow the tablet whole with a glass of water.[1]
  • An FDA-approved levothyroxine label in the US: a single daily dose, preferably on an empty stomach, half an hour to an hour before breakfast.[2]
  • A Turkish patient leaflet in the archive of TİTCK, Turkey's medicines agency: in the morning, at least half an hour before breakfast, on an empty stomach, preferably with some liquid (for example half a glass of water).[3]

All three point the same way, but the details differ. Follow the leaflet in your own box; if you see a contradiction, ask your pharmacist.

Why on an empty stomach?

According to the FDA label, absorption of this hormone from the gut increases when you're fasting, while some foods such as soybeans, and dietary fibre, reduce it.[2] The Turkish leaflet asks you to tell your doctor if you eat soy foods, especially if you've changed how much you eat, because the dose may need adjusting.[3] The FDA label also says the need for a dose adjustment should be assessed if the medicine is regularly taken within an hour of certain foods.[2]

In practical terms: your habit is information that matters for your dose. If you've changed the way you take the medicine (for example, you now take it with breakfast), tell your doctor.

Medicines and supplements

Some medicines and supplements also affect absorption. The FDA label says iron and calcium supplements and antacids (medicines that neutralise stomach acid) can reduce the absorption of levothyroxine, and that it shouldn't be taken within 4 hours of them.[2] The Turkish leaflet asks for a gap of at least 2 hours from medicines containing substances such as antacids, calcium carbonate, iron and sucralfate.[3] The NHS also lists calcium and iron supplements, and antacids and other indigestion treatments, among the products that can interact with levothyroxine.[1] If you take one of these, ask your pharmacist what time to take it.

If you take a supplement containing biotin, tell your doctor before a thyroid blood test. The FDA label advises stopping biotin and biotin-containing supplements at least 2 days before TSH and T4 levels are measured.[2]

If the pharmacy gives you a different box

The Turkish leaflet says that if you need to switch to another product containing levothyroxine, thyroid hormone levels may become unbalanced, that close monitoring is needed during the switch, and that you should talk to your doctor if you have questions.[3] If the pharmacy has given you a different product, tell your doctor.

Building the morning routine: before the coffee

When you build a habit chain, you attach the new behaviour to something you already do every morning; we explain the technique in our article on building medication into your morning routine. With levothyroxine, where the anchor sits matters. Because the NHS says to take it before any drink with caffeine, the anchor can't be "after I've made my coffee"; it has to be a step that comes before the coffee.[1] An example sequence:

  1. When you wake up, take the medicine, kept by your bed, with a glass of water.
  2. Shower, get dressed, get ready for the day: the waiting time your leaflet asks for passes meanwhile.
  3. Then breakfast, and coffee or tea.

Where you keep the medicine is part of the routine too. The FDA label says the tablets should be protected from light and moisture; the Turkish leaflet says to store them below 25°C, in their packaging, out of the sight and reach of children.[2][3] A dry place, such as a bedside drawer that children can't reach, fits both the storage conditions and the "first thing when I wake up" routine better than a steamy bathroom cabinet.

If you take an iron or calcium supplement, ask your pharmacist about moving it to lunchtime or the evening, for example. Each medicine in Dozi has its own reminder time, so setting two separate times is no problem.

What if I forgot this morning?

Here, too, the sources word it differently. The NHS says to take it as soon as you remember, unless it's nearly time for your next dose, in which case skip the missed one, and not to take two doses together to make up for it.[1] The Turkish leaflet in TİTCK's archive says not to take two doses because of the forgotten tablet, and to carry on with the normal daily dose the next day.[3] Your leaflet and your doctor tell you which applies to your medicine. For the general picture, see our article "I missed a dose".

Why the same routine every day?

Levothyroxine is usually a long-term treatment. The NHS says you'll usually need to take it for the rest of your life, and the Turkish leaflet says treatment usually continues for life.[1][3] According to the NHS, regular blood tests check whether you're on the right dose, and your doctor may start you on a low dose and increase it gradually.[1] The FDA label says it may take several weeks before you notice an improvement in symptoms.[2]

So this isn't an "I'll take it when I remember" medicine. It's a habit you'll repeat every day for years. Setting up the routine properly once makes every morning after that easier.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

The Turkish leaflet says levothyroxine treatment should continue during pregnancy, that the dose may need to be increased during this time, and that if you find out you're pregnant during treatment you should talk to your doctor straight away.[3] The FDA label also asks patients to tell their healthcare provider if they are pregnant, breastfeeding or thinking of becoming pregnant.[2] Talk to your doctor about what your routine and your dose should be during this time.
