I Missed a Dose of My Medication. What Should I Do?

The answer depends on the medicine. Here is where the right answer is written down, and who to ask.

← All Articles

Bardino · 15 October 2026

Short answer: take out the patient leaflet that came with your medicine and read the part about what to do if you forget a dose. That is where your answer is. Unless the leaflet or your doctor says otherwise, don't take two doses at once to make up for the one you missed. If you don't have the leaflet, or your situation doesn't match what it says, ask your pharmacist or the doctor who prescribed the medicine.

This article doesn't tell you what to do for any particular medicine. It explains why we can't, why the answer changes from one medicine to the next, and where to find the right answer for yours.

Why does the answer depend on the medicine?

The Specialist Pharmacy Service (SPS), part of the NHS in England, sets out two risks side by side in its guidance for health professionals: taking a medicine at the wrong time may reduce how well it works, and taking the following doses too close together may increase the risk of side effects.[1] Deciding what to do about a missed dose means balancing those two risks, and the balance point is different for every medicine.

The main things that decide it:

  • How many times a day the medicine is taken. Even the general advice from SPS is written separately for medicines taken once or twice a day and for those taken more often.[1]
  • How long until the next dose. The shorter the gap between the moment you remember and your next dose, the closer together the two doses end up.
  • The type of medicine. For some groups of medicines the general rules don't apply at all. We list them below.

Putting official texts side by side shows the difference. The patient leaflet for a betahistine tablet taken twice a day for Ménière's disease (a condition that causes vertigo), published on the website of TİTCK, Turkey's medicines and medical devices agency, says to skip the forgotten dose and take the next one at the usual time.[2] A levothyroxine (thyroid hormone) leaflet on the same site says not to take two doses because of the forgotten tablet, and to carry on with the normal daily dose the next day.[3] The NHS page on levothyroxine says to take it as soon as you remember, unless it's nearly time for your next dose.[4] So the wording can differ between sources even for the same active ingredient. What applies to you is the leaflet from your own box and what your doctor has told you.

Where to look in the leaflet

What goes into the leaflet is set by law. In Turkey, a regulation published in the Official Gazette (Resmî Gazete) in 2017 requires the leaflet to say what to do if one or more doses are not taken and, where relevant, what risks stopping the medicine can bring.[5] In the UK, the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 include among the leaflet's instructions for proper use what to do if one or more doses have not been taken, and the action, if any, to take after an overdose.[6] In both countries, then, the answer to your question has to be in the leaflet.

In the two Turkish leaflets we looked at for this article, the information sits in section 3, "How is ... used?", under three subheadings (translated here):[2][3]

  • "If you use more ... than you should"
  • "If you forget to use ..."
  • "Effects that may occur when treatment with ... is stopped"

A leaflet in English may word these headings differently. Look in the section on how to take or use the medicine.

If you've lost the leaflet, your pharmacist can help, and official copies are online. In Turkey, approved leaflets are published on TİTCK's website; both examples in this article come from there.[2][3] In the UK, the MHRA products website, run by the medicines regulator, lets you find the leaflets provided with medicines.[7] In the US, DailyMed, run by the National Library of Medicine, contains the labeling that companies submit to the FDA.[8]

The rule that applies almost everywhere: no double doses

The SPS guidance says never to take a double dose to make up for a forgotten one, unless a prescriber advises it.[1] Both Turkish leaflets we looked at contain the same sentence: "Do not take a double dose to make up for forgotten doses."[2][3] The NHS page for carers also says it's dangerous to take a double dose later on to make up for a missed one.[9]

Medicines where the general rules don't apply

The SPS guidance gives separate, more cautious advice for the groups below, and says the list is not exhaustive:[1]

  • Medicines for seizures (epilepsy)
  • Contraceptive pills
  • Medicines for Parkinson's disease
  • Insulin
  • Methotrexate taken once a week
  • Warfarin
  • Immunosuppressants and cancer medicines

If you take a medicine from one of these groups

Don't rely on general rules from the internet, this article included. Check your leaflet and ask the doctor, clinic or pharmacist who looks after you. SPS itself advises people on insulin to follow the instructions of the team that manages their diabetes, people on Parkinson's medicines to contact their Parkinson's nurse or clinic, and people on warfarin to contact their anticoagulant clinic if they are worried or have missed more than one dose.[1]

If you took too much by mistake

Read the leaflet's section on using more than you should. Both leaflets we looked at say to talk to a doctor or pharmacist if this happens.[2][3]

In Turkey, the National Poison Information Centre (UZEM), part of the Ministry of Health, advises doctors, other health workers and the public on poisons and poisoning around the clock, 7 days a week, on the 114 line.[10] Many other countries have a poisons centre too. Poisons centres answer questions about exposure to chemicals, including medicines, and WHO says most of them give emergency advice to the public as well as to health professionals. Not every country has one, though: as of 1 January 2023, only 47% of WHO Member States did. WHO's Global Health Observatory links to a world directory of poisons centres.[11][12] If you can't find one where you live, call your doctor or pharmacist. Keeping the box with you when you call makes it easier to give the name and dose correctly. If someone is unwell, call your local emergency number.

What to tell the pharmacist or doctor

In Turkey, TİTCK's Good Pharmacy Practice Guidelines expect pharmacists to tell patients, and the people who give them their medicines, about the dose, the dosing times and the foods and other medicines to avoid around a dose, and to ask which medicines the patient is already taking.[13] Wherever you are, you'll get a quicker and more accurate answer if you have this ready:

  • The name and dose of the medicine (better still, the box)
  • When the missed dose was due, and the time now
  • When the next dose is due
  • Whether you missed one dose, or several doses or days
  • Any other medicines, vitamins and supplements you take

Next time: missing fewer doses

The leaflet and a health professional tell you the right step after a missed dose. What is in your hands is missing fewer doses in the first place. Approved leaflets say so too: the betahistine leaflet we looked at says that taking the tablets at the same time every day will also help you remember to take them.[2]

  • Link the medicine to something you already do every day. We explain how in our article on building medication into your morning routine.
  • If your days are irregular, a place can work better than a clock as a trigger: location-based reminders (a Dozi Ekstra feature).
  • Running out is another way doses get missed. With stock tracking (also Dozi Ekstra) you can get to the pharmacy before the box is empty.
  • If you take several medicines, work out the timings with your pharmacist rather than on your own: our guide to keeping track of multiple medications.
