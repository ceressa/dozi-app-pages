Keeping Track of Multiple Medications: A Guide to Polypharmacy

A complete medicines list, a review with your pharmacist, and a daily schedule worked out with a health professional.

← All Articles

Bardino · 15 October 2026

If you take several medicines, or organise them for a family member, the foundation of keeping track is a complete, up-to-date medicines list: the name, dose and timing of each medicine, what it's for and who prescribed it. The second step is to go through that list with your pharmacist or doctor. Decisions such as combining dose times, or adding or dropping a medicine, are made with them, not on your own.

What is polypharmacy?

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines polypharmacy as the use of multiple medicines at the same time. There is no standard definition, but it often means the routine use of five or more medicines. Prescription and over-the-counter medicines count, and so do traditional and complementary products.[1]

Taking many medicines is not wrong in itself. The WHO report gives an example: preventing another heart attack in someone who has had one can require four different classes of medicine.[1] The report distinguishes "appropriate" from "inappropriate" polypharmacy. It is appropriate when every medicine is prescribed for a specific aim agreed with the person, those aims are being achieved (or are likely to be), the treatment has been arranged to keep the risk of adverse reactions low, and the person is willing and able to take the medicines as intended. It is inappropriate when one or more medicines are not needed or no longer needed, don't achieve their aim, cause adverse reactions or put the person at high risk of them, or when the person is not willing or able to take them as intended.[1]

The same report lists the risks: drug-drug interactions, a higher risk of falls, adverse drug reactions, cognitive impairment, not taking medicines as intended, and poor nutrition. People over 65 and people living in care homes are among the groups most exposed to them.[1]

1. Make a complete medicines list

WHO says a medicines list kept by the patient, on paper or electronic and sometimes called a medication passport, can help get the most out of their medicines, and that an up-to-date list can also help when they move from one care setting to another.[1] Put these on the list:

  • The name on the box and the active ingredient
  • The dose, how many times a day, and at what times
  • Whether it's taken with food or on an empty stomach, or any other special instruction
  • What it's for and who prescribed it
  • When you started it
  • Over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, supplements and herbal products you take
  • Known drug allergies and any reactions to medicines you've had before[3]

Don't leave out over-the-counter products. WHO says patients should be encouraged and supported to tell their health professionals about all the medicines they take, including over-the-counter, traditional and complementary ones.[1] Patient leaflets ask for the same thing: a Turkish leaflet we looked at asks you to tell the doctor you are taking that medicine whenever you see a doctor or go to hospital, and to tell your doctor or pharmacist about any other prescription or non-prescription medicines you use.[2]

2. Go through the list with a health professional

NICE, the body that issues health guidance in England, recommends considering a "structured medication review", when there is a clear purpose for it, for groups including people taking multiple medicines, people with long-term conditions and older people. The review takes into account the person's views and understanding of their medicines (and, where appropriate, those of their family or carers), their concerns, questions or problems with the medicines, all the prescription, over-the-counter and complementary medicines they take and what each is for, how safe and effective the medicines are, the risk of adverse reactions, and any monitoring needed. According to NICE, the review may be led by a pharmacist, for example.[3]

WHO, for its part, names the points at which polypharmacy should be looked at: when a new medicine is started, during medication reviews, and at care transitions, when a person moves from one care setting to another. Among the situations where the report says a review may be especially useful are taking 10 or more medicines, taking high-risk medicines and living in a care home.[1]

In Turkey, the pharmacist's role here is set out in the Good Pharmacy Practice Guidelines of TİTCK, the country's medicines agency: the pharmacist informs the patient, and the person who gives them their medicines, about the dose, dosing times and the foods and other medicines to avoid around a dose, and asks which medicines the patient is taking.[4] You don't need to wait for a problem before taking your list to the pharmacist. Questions you could ask:

  • Which of these medicines can be taken at the same time, and which need a gap between them?
  • Which should be taken on an empty stomach, and which with food?
  • Are there medicines on this list that might interact with each other?
  • What should I do if I miss a dose? (The general picture: I missed a dose of my medication. What should I do?)
  • Which symptoms should I report to my doctor?

3. Group the times, but not on your own

Settling on two or three fixed times a day, rather than taking medicines at six different times, makes tracking easier. WHO also says that practical problems with taking medicines can be addressed through practical changes to the regimen.[1] But a health professional should make that change, because some medicines have to be taken apart from each other or apart from food.

One example is levothyroxine, the thyroid hormone replacement. In the US, an FDA-approved label for it says to take it at least 4 hours before or after drugs that interfere with its absorption, and not within 4 hours of iron and calcium supplements or antacids.[5] A Turkish patient leaflet in TİTCK's archive says to leave at least 2 hours between it and medicines containing antacids, calcium carbonate or iron.[6] The required gap differs even between sources, so don't decide to "take them all at nine in the morning" without asking your pharmacist. For more detail, see our article on thyroid medication.

Weekly pill organisers

WHO says multi-compartment pill boxes are not suitable for every patient and every medicine, and may not give enough information about the dose; the NHS also notes that these boxes are not suitable for every type of medicine.[1][7] Ask your pharmacist before you use one.

4. Supplies and prescription dates

Five different boxes run out on five different days. Seeing when each will run out, all in one place, makes pharmacy visits and prescription renewals easier to plan. We cover ways to keep count of the tablets you have left in our stock tracking article.

5. Record every change on the list

A new medicine, a change of dose, a hospital discharge: the list only helps if it's current. NICE describes "medicines reconciliation" as identifying an accurate list of a person's current medicines, comparing it with the list in use, recognising any discrepancies and documenting any changes; over-the-counter and complementary medicines are included.[3] In practice, this means comparing the medicines list you're given when you leave hospital with your list at home, and asking about any difference you don't understand.

If a new symptom has started and a medicine was added recently, write it down. We explain how to keep a record of side effects in our side effect tracking article. In Turkey, TİTCK's guidelines also expect pharmacists to tell patients that suspected adverse reactions should be reported to TÜFAM, the Turkish Pharmacovigilance Centre.[4] In the UK, NICE's guidance refers to reporting adverse drug reactions through the Yellow Card scheme.[3] If you live somewhere else, ask your pharmacist how suspected side effects are reported where you are.

Managing several medicines in Dozi

In Dozi you can set a separate reminder time and dose information for each medicine, and with Dozi Ekstra stock tracking as well; reminders can be daily, weekly or on a custom schedule. For stock tracking, you enter the number of tablets you have when you add the medicine; Dozi counts down with each dose taken and warns you when stock runs low (FAQ).

Dozi Ekstra also lets you add a medicine by showing its box to the camera (Dozi Ekstra). Check what you add this way against your prescription: patient leaflets say the doctor will set the dose, so the information on the box may not be your dose.[2][6]

One limit to know about: the free plan covers up to 10 medicines; for more than that, and for stock tracking, you need Dozi Ekstra (Pricing).

Dozi keeps your list in order and reminds you; it doesn't judge whether your medicines are suitable to take together. That is your pharmacist's and your doctor's job. If you manage a family member's medicines from a distance, see our guide for remote caregivers too.
