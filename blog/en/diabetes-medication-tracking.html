Diabetes Medication Tracking: A Guide to Type 2 Diabetes Adherence

Taking your medication as prescribed is an important part of keeping your blood sugar under control.

← All Articles

According to the International Diabetes Federation, 589 million adults aged 20-79 live with diabetes, and the number is projected to reach 853 million by 2050. Managing blood sugar well lowers the chance of complications. Yet many people with type 2 diabetes do not take their medication as prescribed: across 27 studies in a 2015 review, the share of adherent patients ranged from 38.5% to 93.1%, and only 6 studies found 80% or more of patients adherent.

The "It Doesn't Matter If I Skip" Misconception

This is one of the most common traps diabetes patients fall into. In Type 2 diabetes, the effects of medication are usually not felt immediately. You do not feel "better" the morning you take your metformin. You do not feel "worse" the morning you skip it. This silent nature gives your brain room to question whether the medication is truly necessary.

The reality is that diabetes medication benefits manifest over months and years, not hours. A single missed dose may seem insignificant on its own, but this habit can gradually erode your medication adherence entirely over time.

⚠️ The Silent Danger

Type 2 diabetes raises the risk of long-term problems with the heart and blood vessels, eyes, kidneys, nerves and feet. The NHS notes that managing your blood sugar as well as you can lowers the chances of these complications, and that regular diabetes check-ups help find them early. Diabetes itself can go unnoticed: in Turkey's national TURDEP-II survey, almost half of the adults found to have diabetes had not been diagnosed before.

The Connection Between HbA1c and Consistent Medication Use

HbA1c (A1C) is a blood test that reflects your average blood sugar level over roughly the past 3 months. In the UKPDS 35 study of people with type 2 diabetes, each 1 percentage point lower HbA1c was associated with 21% fewer diabetes-related complications, 14% fewer heart attacks and 37% fewer microvascular complications, with no threshold below which the benefit stopped. This was an observational analysis, so it shows an association rather than the effect of any single treatment.

Diet, physical activity and medication all play a part, and the right combination is a decision for you and your diabetes team. What medication can do depends on it being taken as prescribed.

Reminder Strategies by Medication Type

Metformin (Oral Antidiabetic)

According to the NHS, most people take metformin 1 to 3 times a day with food; follow the instructions on your own prescription and leaflet. With Dozi, you can set separate reminders for each meal and add a "take with food" note to the medication.

Insulin Injections

Insulin schedules depend on the type of insulin and on you: some are taken once a day, others with meals. Follow the plan your diabetes team gave you. Dozi's location-based reminder can alert you about your evening insulin when you arrive home. Stock tracking lets you know in advance when your insulin pen will run out.

Combination Therapies

Many Type 2 diabetes patients use multiple medications: metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, or GLP-1 agonists. Each has different timing and intake rules. Dozi allows you to manage all your medications in one place and set individual reminders for each.
